What the map shows

Each dot is a NASA VIIRS 375 m active-fire alert in the current fire season (). The newest month is bright yellow and earlier months fade through pink and violet to blue. One fire can produce several alerts over several days, and smoke or cloud can hide fires, so alert counts show fire activity, not burned area.

Site outlines are coloured by type, not by fire level: green for protected and conserved areas (CCAs, WMAs, parks, sanctuary), white (blue on the street map) for Key Biodiversity Areas, and dashed for proposed areas. How a site compares with earlier years is shown in its panel and in the Sites table.

Comparing years

For each site and province, alerts so far this season are compared with the same calendar period in every year since , using the Global Forest Watch Data API (the data behind PNG DAMS). Each site boundary is registered as a GFW geostore, so its PNG DAMS report opens for that exact boundary.

Satellites. The Global Forest Watch VIIRS dataset is the VNP14IMGT product from the Suomi NPP satellite only. Alerts from NOAA-20 and NOAA-21, launched in 2017 and 2022, are not included, so the record since 2012 comes from one sensor and adding satellites does not inflate recent years.

Confidence. Each alert has a confidence of low, nominal or high. Tick "Exclude low-confidence alerts" to drop low-confidence alerts from the map, the panels, the charts and the Sites table. In tests for Western Province, excluding them changed totals by 7–14% and barely changed comparisons between years, so all alerts are shown by default, as on Global Forest Watch. PDF reports, printed maps and firebreak counts use all alerts.

Around each site

  • Alerts outside a site are counted 0– km and 0– km from its boundary, measured in the site's own UTM zone. These counts include alerts in neighbouring provinces.
  • Open a site and use "Show alerts" to see only the alerts inside it, or only those within km. Every site also has printable maps (JPG) of both views and a four-page PDF report.

Firebreak planning

Each site boundary is cut into sections of about 1 km (longer for the largest sites, up to 300 sections). For every section the dashboard counts in how many years since VIIRS alerts fell in the edge zone, from km outside to km inside the boundary. Sections where fire comes back most years are where fire most often reaches the site, so they are the first places to consider for firebreaks, early-season burning or fire patrols.

This is a screening tool. Check each section on the ground with landowners and rangers: slope, wind, grassland and garden edges, and roads, rivers and bare ground that already stop fire. Sites without a surveyed boundary (approximate circles) give indicative results only.

Nearest water

Click a fire alert to see the nearest mapped river or lake, its distance and direction, and a line to it. Rivers come from HydroRIVERS, which maps streams with at least 10 km² of upstream catchment; lakes come from HydroLAKES (10 ha or larger). Line positions are approximate (often within 500 m), and creeks, springs, tanks and village water points are not mapped. Small streams can dry up in a drought, so confirm water on the ground before planning a response.

Other measures

  • Integrated alerts (GLAD-L, GLAD-S2, RADD) count 10–30 m alert pixels, not hectares, and rise in dry, smoky years.
  • Tree cover loss is Hansen/UMD annual loss at more than 30% canopy.
  • District fire density is alerts per 100 km² on OCHA COD-AB districts (PNG National Statistical Office, 2019).
  • Proposed Mt Dorohere, Yasina, Artrohialika Lulume and Mt Giluwe, and the Kuk site, have no surveyed boundary yet, so each is a circle of its stated area around its GPS point.

How it updates

A scheduled job runs twice a day. It checks Global Forest Watch for new alerts and, when there are any, rebuilds every number, map and PDF report. The firebreak baseline is extended once a year when a new season starts. Last update: .

Limitations

  • A fire alert is a hot 375 m pixel seen by the satellite at about 1:30 am and 1:30 pm. One fire can produce many alerts, and small, brief or cloud-covered fires are missed.
  • Alert positions are pixel centres, so an alert can be up to about 300 m from the actual fire, which matters for alerts close to a boundary.
  • Site boundaries come from different sources and vary in accuracy.
